How Do You Fix a Leaking Shower Faucet in Mill Creek, WA?

A shower that drips after you turn it off wastes water and wears out the tub finish, and fixing it usually means replacing one small internal part. This guide for Mill Creek, WA homes explains how to find which type of valve you have and what to change.

The work is not hard, but the right part depends on your brand and age of valve. A wet ceiling or damp wall nearby is a different problem entirely, and that one calls for a plumber.

Two kinds of valve, two kinds of fix

A single-handle shower usually has a cartridge. It is a plastic or brass cylinder inside the valve body that slides or turns to mix hot and cold. When its rubber seals wear, the shower drips or the temperature drifts.

Older homes, and many two-handle sets, use compression stems. Each handle turns a threaded stem that presses a rubber washer onto a seat. When the washer hardens, water squeezes past, and you hear a steady drip even with the handle fully closed.

Identify the brand before buying parts

Cartridges are not interchangeable. Look for a brand name on the handle, the trim plate, or the spout. Take the handle off and note any model number on the cartridge. A photo of the old cartridge, taken from the side, helps the parts counter match it.

If you cannot find a name, bring the old cartridge in and compare it directly. Many brands share similar shapes with different spline counts and lengths, so a close match is not a match. Pressing a wrong part into the valve body can crack it.

Shut off the water and prepare

  • Turn off the water at the fixture stops if the valve has them, or at the main valve if it does not
  • Open the shower to confirm the water has stopped and the line is drained
  • Plug the tub drain with a rag so a dropped screw cannot disappear
  • Lay a towel in the tub bottom to protect the finish
  • Gather an Allen key set, a screwdriver, pliers, and a replacement cartridge or washers

Many shower valves have no separate shutoffs. In that case the main valve is the only option. If the main will not close, or it leaks at the stem, stop and call. A stuck main is not a place to force.

Replace the part, step by step

  1. Remove the handle. Look for a set screw under a small cap on the handle, or a screw behind a trim cover. Loosen it with an Allen key and pull the handle straight off.
  2. Take off the trim. Unscrew the escutcheon, the round plate on the wall, and look for caulk around its edge. Cut the caulk lightly with a utility knife before pulling.
  3. Remove the retaining clip or nut. A cartridge is held by a clip or a bonnet nut. Pull the clip with pliers, or unscrew the nut. Take a photo first so you know how it sat.
  4. Pull the cartridge or stem. A cartridge may need a puller tool or a gentle twist with pliers. A compression stem unscrews counterclockwise with a deep socket or an adjustable wrench.
  5. Install the new part. Line up the notches, press the cartridge in until it seats, then restore the clip. For a stem, replace the washer or stem and seat, then thread it back in.
  6. Reassemble and test. Refit the trim and handle, turn the water back on slowly, and test hot and cold. Watch for drips at the trim, and seal the escutcheon with caulk only after everything is dry.

Mistakes that cause a second repair

Breaking the old part while pulling it is the top issue. A cartridge that is stuck with mineral buildup can snap, leaving half in the valve body. Do not pry with a screwdriver. If it will not release after gentle twisting, stop before you damage the body.

Another is installing a cartridge rotated the wrong way, which swaps hot and cold or limits the temperature. Match the ear or notch positions to the old one. Also avoid using plumber's tape on parts that are not meant to be taped, and never overtighten a bonnet nut.

When the wall must be opened, or you should call

If the valve body itself leaks, if the threads are damaged, or if the stem seat is rough beyond smoothing, the valve needs replacing. That means opening the wall from the back or cutting the tile. Do not try that as a first DIY job.

A stain on the ceiling below, a damp wall outside the shower, or a musty smell can mean a leak behind the wall. That is a job for leak detection, using moisture meters and thermal equipment to find the source before opening anything. Water behind a wall damages framing quietly.

Shower faucet questions

Why does my shower drip only after hot showers?

Heat can swell a worn seal or loosen the cartridge slightly. A new cartridge usually ends it. If the drip stops after a minute, the water in the pipe is just draining.

Can I replace a cartridge without shutting off the main?

Only if the valve has its own stops. Many do not. Without them, the main valve is the only safe way to work.

How do I know if the leak is in the wall?

Water stains on the ceiling or wall nearby, a soft or bubbled patch, or a meter that moves with everything off are warning signs. Turn off the water and call.

Is it worth repairing an old shower valve or replacing it?

If a cartridge or washer is available and the body is sound, repair is wiser. If the body is corroded or the parts are discontinued, replacement saves repeat visits.
