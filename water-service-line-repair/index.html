Emergency Water Service Line Repair in Mill Creek, WA

No water at all, a lawn turning into a pond, or a meter that will not stop spinning all point to trouble in the line that feeds your house. Close the main valve at the house if it turns, avoid driving or walking over a soft wet patch, and call our 24/7 line. We book same-day appointments when available.

A water service line is the buried pipe that carries water from the meter to your house. Water service line repair in Mill Creek, WA means locating a leak or break in that run, deciding between a spot repair and a longer replacement, and getting water back on safely. We also look at low pressure and no-water complaints, because the cause often sits in the same pipe.

When a service line problem is an emergency

A broken service line can send a steady stream of water into your yard, undermine a driveway or walkway, and run your meter day and night. Water that surfaces near the foundation or flows into a crawlspace is urgent. So is losing water entirely, especially with children, elderly family members or a medical need in the home.

We answer around the clock, so a Saturday night break is not a reason to wait until Monday. We book same-day appointments when available, including after hours, but we do not promise an arrival time. We will tell you the plan and the price before work starts, and we explain what the water utility may need to do on its side.

Find the house main

Locate the shutoff where the line enters the house and turn it off. This stops water inside, but it will not stop a leak outside, since the break may be before the valve.

Look at the meter

With the house main closed, see whether the meter still moves. If it does, the loss is on the line between the meter and the house. Note the reading and time.

Mark the wet spot

Place a flag or stake at the soggy or sunken area, and keep kids, pets and vehicles away from soft ground.

Do not dig yet

Call 811 before any digging so buried utilities are marked. Hitting a gas, electric or fiber line is far worse than a water leak.

Service line leak between the meter and the house

This is the repair people call about most. Signs include a wet or unusually green patch of ground, a sunken spot in a lawn or driveway, a hissing sound near the entry point, a falling pressure reading and a bill that suddenly doubles. Sometimes a leak never surfaces at all, because the water drains into gravel or soil.

We confirm the leak with a meter test, then trace it. Our leak detection methods, including acoustic listening and tracer gas, narrow the break to a section of pipe. Plastic lines are quiet, so they can be harder to hear than metal, and we tell you honestly if we narrow it to a stretch instead of a point.

Older pipe, shifting soil, tree roots and joints made with worn fittings are the usual causes. In neighborhoods with mature evergreen and broadleaf trees, roots can lean on a buried line for years. The first-phase Mill Creek homes, built from 1976 and mostly finished by about 1983, may have original service lines that are now over four decades old.

Low water pressure

Low pressure has several causes, and the service line is only one of them. A partial leak in the buried line, a partly closed or failing main valve, a clogged pressure regulator, corroded interior pipe or a single clogged aerator can all weaken the flow. We start by checking whether the problem is the whole house or one fixture.

  1. Compare fixtures. If only one faucet is weak, the cause is local, such as a clogged aerator or a worn cartridge. If every fixture is weak, the issue is upstream.
  2. Check the main valve. A gate valve that is only partly open will restrict flow. Make sure it is fully open.
  3. Look at the meter. A meter moving with all fixtures off suggests a leak, which can also drain pressure.
  4. Call us. We test pressure with a gauge at a hose bib and look at the regulator, the valve and the incoming line.

We do not state what pressure your street delivers. If the low pressure follows a utility outage or street work, ask your water utility about conditions in your area. If the gauge reads normal at the meter but poor in the house, the problem is on our side of the line, and we can usually find it.

No water at the house

When the water goes off completely, start with the simple checks. Look at neighbors and see if the outage is wider. Check whether the main valve was bumped closed and whether the meter box is flooded or the meter dial is stuck. Make sure a bill or account hold is not the reason, since the utility may have turned service off.

If the neighbors have water and you do not, a break in your line, a failed valve or a frozen section after a cold night are all possible. Water may still reach the house but be blocked by a clogged pressure regulator or a failed pump on a private system. A plumber can isolate the problem by testing at the meter, the main and the first fixture.

Repair or replace the service line

A spot repair is the right call when one joint or a short section has failed and the rest of the line is sound. Replacement makes more sense when the pipe is old, has leaked more than once, is a material that is known to age poorly, or has been repaired several times. We explain what we see and let you decide.

Long private runs and townhouse lines

Some lots have a long private run between the meter and the house, and a longer run is the harder case. A long line means more joints, more soil movement and more places for a leak to hide. Searching and repairing takes longer, and the cost of a full replacement rises with the footage.

Townhomes and condos can have shared or neighboring lines that complicate things. If your line crosses a neighbor's lot or a shared easement, tell us up front, because it affects who must approve the work. A homeowners association may also need to be notified before digging.

Suspected lead service line

Some older service lines in the country were made of lead in earlier decades. We cannot tell you the material by looking at a house. The pipe material can be verified by exposing the line near the meter or the house entry and checking it. Ask your water utility whether it keeps records, and check your latest water quality report.

We do not make health claims about lead, and we are not a testing lab. If you want the pipe identified before you plan a replacement, say so on the call and we will tell you what a visual check can and cannot show.

Well-to-house lines on the rural fringe

A few properties around the edges of the area are on wells instead of a city water system. A leak or break in the line from the well to the house, or in the line to an outbuilding, is something we can discuss, but scope varies. Pump, pressure tank and water quality work may call for a well specialist. Ask the dispatcher whether your setup is within what we handle.

Who owns what at the meter

People often ask whether the meter box and the pipe next to it belong to them or the utility. That is set by your water utility, and the answer differs from place to place. Ask your water utility which side of the meter is theirs and which is yours. We then work on the part that belongs to you and tell you when the utility needs to be called.

The City of Mill Creek's utilities page lists two water and wastewater providers, Alderwood Water and Wastewater District and Silver Lake Water and Sewer District. Which one serves a given address depends on where the home sits, so check your latest bill or call the provider to confirm. Then ask where its responsibility ends and yours begins.

Water service line questions from Mill Creek homeowners

How much does water service line repair cost?

It depends on the length, depth, what is on top of the line, pipe material and repair method. We give a clear price before work starts. Surface restoration, permits and utility fees can be separate.

Do you handle water service line repair emergencies at night or on weekends in Mill Creek?

Yes. The 24/7 line takes calls for line breaks, flooding yards and no-water situations. We book same-day appointments when available, but we do not promise an arrival time. We will explain the plan and the price before work starts.

How do I know the leak is in my service line?

Close the house main and watch the meter. If the dial still moves, water is escaping between the meter and the shutoff. A wet patch in the yard, a sunken spot or a drop in pressure supports that.

Can you find the leak without digging up the whole yard?

We use listening equipment and sometimes tracer gas to narrow the location first, so digging is limited to the right spot. We cannot promise an exact point on every line, especially plastic pipe.

Should I repair or replace my line?

If one joint failed in otherwise good pipe, a repair is reasonable. If the line is old, has leaked before or is corroded in several places, replacement often costs less over time. We show you what we find.

Can you tell if my service line is lead?

The pipe material can be verified by exposing and checking the line. We do not make health claims. Ask your water utility about its records and read your latest water quality report.

Is the meter box my responsibility or the utility's?

Ask your water utility which side of the meter belongs to them. We handle the part that is yours.

Do I need to call 811 before you dig?

Yes, anyone digging should call 811 first so buried lines are marked. Tell us if you have done that, and we will confirm the marks before the work.

I own a rental. Can you coordinate with my tenant?

Yes. Give us the tenant's contact and your approval for the price, and we schedule around their access and tell you what we find.
